The seven-point stock check

Seven questions investor William O'Neil used to separate stocks that were genuinely growing from ones that only looked cheap. Go find the real numbers for a company you are curious about, fill them in, and see where it holds up and where it does not. The point is the hunt for the data, not the tally at the bottom.

C

Current quarterly earnings

Earnings per share up 25% or more against the same quarter one year earlier. Rising sales alongside rising earnings is the confirmation, because a company can lift earnings for a quarter by cutting costs, and that trick only works once.

Where to look  The company's most recent quarterly earnings release, or its 10-Q filing on the SEC's EDGAR database.

Enter the two figures above.

A

Annual earnings growth

One good quarter can be luck. O'Neil wanted annual earnings growing 25% or more a year across three years, and a return on equity of 17% or better, which shows the company turns shareholder money into profit efficiently rather than just getting bigger.

Where to look  The annual report or 10-K. Return on equity is listed on most stock summary pages.

Enter the two figures above.

N

Something new

A new product, service, or leadership team gives the price somewhere to go. O'Neil paired that with a rule most beginners find backwards: buy as a stock pushes into new high ground, not when it is sitting near its lows looking like a bargain.

Where to look  Company press releases and recent news for the catalyst. Any price chart for the 52-week-high.

Tick whichever apply.

S

Supply and demand

Price moves when buying overwhelms available shares. Look for volume at least 40% above its 50-day average on the days the stock jumps, which says large buyers are involved rather than a handful of small trades.

Where to look  The volume bars under any price chart. Shares outstanding appears on the company's stock summary page.

Enter the volume figure. Shares outstanding adds context.

L

Leader, not laggard

Investors tend to buy the cheaper, weaker company in a hot industry and call it value. O'Neil found the opposite worked: own the strongest one or two names in a strong group. A stock well ahead of the S&P 500 over twelve months is the rough test.

Where to look  A one-year performance chart with the S&P 500 overlaid. Most charting sites let you add a comparison line.

Enter both percentages.

I

Institutional sponsorship

Funds and pensions move enough money to carry a price for months. You want their numbers rising quarter over quarter. Near-total institutional ownership works against you though, since it can mean everyone who was going to buy already has.

Where to look  The institutional ownership section of most stock summary pages, or 13F filings on EDGAR.

Enter the ownership figure and the trend.

M

Market direction

The one that overrides the other six. O'Neil's research found roughly three of every four stocks follow the general market, so a company that passes every test above will still tend to fall when the whole market is falling.

Where to look  The trend of the major indexes over recent weeks, and whether they are holding above or breaking below their 50-day moving averages.

Select the current condition.

Where it stands

The market rule overrides the rest. Whatever the six company criteria say above, O'Neil's own method calls for standing aside while the general market is falling and waiting for the indexes to turn. This is the point of the exercise that costs people the most when they skip it.

This worksheet is a classroom exercise for learning how to read company filings and price data. It is not investment advice, not a recommendation to buy or sell anything, and a high count here does not mean a stock will rise. Passing all seven points has never guaranteed a profitable investment and never will.

The seven criteria are drawn from the CAN SLIM investing framework developed by William J. O'Neil and published in How to Make Money in Stocks.
